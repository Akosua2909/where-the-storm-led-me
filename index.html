Read Chapter 1: The Weight of Red Dirt

Red dirt. Thick as dried blood, hard as iron, and entirely uncooperative when you were trying to make a mud-brick oven look respectable enough to bake family bread. Otutuwa scraped the blade of her rusted kitchen knife against the baked clay wall, her knuckles raw and gray with cold wood ash. A sudden gust of dry Harmattan wind whipped through the open compound, carrying with it a fine, gritty dust that instantly found its way into her eyes and nostrils. She blinked hard, clearing tears that rose from the sting, and set the knife down beside the small clay bowl she had been using to mix the soil. The bowl was cracked, the rim chipped, and her fingers ached from the work. But she had already spent too much time staring at the wall to stop now. Behind her, the compound was alive with the sounds of morning: chickens scratching in the dirt, a goat tethered near the back fence, and the low murmur of her mother speaking to the neighbor over a bucket of washing. Somewhere beyond the houses, a radio crackled with a familiar tune from the market women on their way to town. It was the kind of morning that made the world seem large and cruel and full of impossible things. Otutuwa lifted her eyes toward the sky. The sun was still low, a thin gold coin rising over the roofs of Suhum, but already it promised heat. The day would be long, and her body was not ready for it. Still, there was no room for weakness in a home like hers. She was seventeen, though sometimes she felt older than the women who had birthed children before her. Her shoulders carried the weight of chores and worry, of late-night whispering and early-morning labor. The family depended on her in ways they never said out loud. A voice broke the silence behind her. “Otutuwa.” She turned. Her younger brother, Kofi, stood by the doorway with his school satchel hanging from one shoulder. His uniform shirt was missing a button, and his shoes were held together by thread. “Are you done?” he asked. She looked at the wall, then at him. “Almost.” He glanced toward the sky. “If we leave now, we might still make it before the bell.” Otutuwa smiled without much joy. “Then you should go.” He did not move. Instead, he stepped closer and asked quietly, “Are you still thinking about it?” She did not answer immediately. There was no use pretending she could hide the thoughts. They had been with her since the night before, coiled inside her chest like a storm waiting behind a mountain. The family had spoken of the flood in hushed voices. About the water rising in the lower quarters near the stream. About the roofs that had collapsed and the people who had lost everything. It had happened far away, but the news had traveled fast and left behind a kind of dread that refused to leave. “Tomorrow,” her father had said, “you and your mother will go to the market and sell the goats. The money must be made before the rains begin.” He had not looked at Otutuwa when he said it. It was always easier to speak in practical terms than to say what he really feared. That was the problem with her life: it was all practicality, all survival, all motion without rest. She bent to pick up the rusted knife and wiped the dust from her hands. “You should go, Kofi.” His face fell, but he nodded. “I’ll tell Mama you’ll be along soon.” When he was gone, the compound seemed even quieter. The wind moved through the grass, and the air carried the faint scent of smoke from the cooking fire. Her mother was humming now, a low tune she used when she was trying not to cry. Otutuwa stood alone for a moment and let her eyes drift over the small world she had been born into. It was not much. A half-finished clay house. A fence with a broken gate. A few pepper plants and a bent mango tree. Yet it had been the only place she had ever known as home, and the only thing holding her there was the weight of responsibility. She closed her fingers around the knife handle. If she was ever going to leave this place, it would not be by accident. Not by chance. Not by prayer. It would be by force. And that thought, more than any other, sat heavy in her chest like a buried stone.
